## Key Question 1: What do Lossie 2001, Gillessen-Kaesbach 1999, Mertz 2014, Keute 2021, Bindels-de Heus 2020, den Besten 2021, Larson 2015 and Prasad 2018 (plus related cohorts) report on UPD vs. deletion, and on adult behaviour and medication use?

### Takeaway
Paternal UPD (≈2–7 % of AS) gives a milder phenotype overall than the common 5–7 Mb deletion (≈70 %). It brings fewer and later seizures (often none), less microcephaly and hypopigmentation, better motor skills, and more communicative and adaptive skill. It is consistently linked to **higher BMI/obesity and more hyperphagia**. In Keute 2021, UPD was more impaired than imprinting-defect (ID) and UBE3A-mutation cases but less impaired than deletion. In adults, behavioural problems are common across genotypes: anxiety, separation distress with a preferred caregiver, aggression maintained by attention, sleep problems, constipation and overweight. Anxiety behaviours are reported **more often in non-deletion and older individuals**. In the only large European adult cohort, only a minority with behavioural problems took psychotropics (16 %, mostly risperidone). Melatonin is the main sleep drug.

### Cited Findings

**Genotype frequencies**
- Large maternal deletion 15q11–q13 ≈70 % of AS; paternal UPD ≈2–7 % (figures vary by source: 2–3 % / 2–7 %); imprinting defect ≈2–7 %. Sources disagree, so cite a range. — [Keute et al. 2021, Mol Psychiatry 26:3625–3633, doi:10.1038/s41380-020-0858-6](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41380-020-0858-6); [Genes 2021 review "Genotype–Phenotype Correlations in AS", doi:10.3390/genes12070987](https://doi.org/10.3390/genes12070987); [Duis et al. 2022 SoC PDF](https://angelman.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/standards-of-care-for-angelman-syndrome.pdf)
- A natural-history cohort of 286 patients: class II deletion 31.1 %, class I 23.8 %, unspecified deletion 10.8 %, atypical deletion 3.8 %, **UPD 8.7 %**, ID 7.7 %, UBE3A mutation 11.2 % (secondary). — [Dovepress review "Angelman syndrome: review of clinical and molecular aspects"](https://www.dovepress.com/angelman-syndrome-review-of-clinical-and-molecular-aspects-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-TACG)
- Danish population data: 51 genetically confirmed AS cases, birth incidence ≈1:24,580; 36 of 51 had a deletion (cohort). — [Mertz et al. 2013, Am J Med Genet A, doi:10.1002/ajmg.a.36058](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ajmg.a.36058)

**Lossie et al. 2001** (cohort; J Med Genet 38(12):834–845; doi:10.1136/jmg.38.12.834; PMID 11748306)
- 104 patients (93 families) with a classic AS phenotype were assigned to molecular classes I–V (deletion, UPD, ID, UBE3A mutation, unknown) and assessed on 22 clinical variables. The authors **could not clinically distinguish UPD from ID** and merged them into one phenotype group, giving four groups. — [PubMed 11748306](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11748306); [SUNY Buffalo record](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/distinct-phenotypes-distinguish-the-molecular-classes-of-angelman/)
- (secondary) UPD patients "appear to have better physical growth and fewer movement abnormalities, ataxia and seizures and are less likely to have microcephaly than the other classes." — [Dagli/Williams review "Molecular and Clinical Aspects of AS", PMC3366701](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3366701/); [GIM review "Clinical and genetic aspects of AS"](https://www.nature.com/articles/gim201062)
- Relative microcephaly in 80 % of all patients, most common with deletions (per OMIM summary of Lossie). — [OMIM #105830](https://omim.org/entry/105830)

**Gillessen-Kaesbach et al. 1999** (case series; Eur J Hum Genet 7:638–644; Essen)
- "A previously unrecognised phenotype characterised by obesity, muscular hypotonia, and ability to speak in patients with AS caused by an imprinting defect." Two large families carried an AS imprinting mutation. Affected members showed obesity, muscular hypotonia and milder intellectual disability, some with speech. The authors suggested an incomplete imprinting defect or cellular mosaicism as the cause. **This paper is about imprinting defects (ID), not UPD.** It is often cited for "non-deletion obesity". — [Eur J Hum Genet article](https://www.nature.com/articles/5200362); [OMIM #105830](https://omim.org/entry/105830); [Univ. Lübeck record](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/en/publications/a-previously-unrecognised-phenotype-characterised-by-obesity-musc/)

**Mertz et al. 2014a** (cohort; Res Dev Disabil 35(7):1742–1747; doi:10.1016/j.ridd.2014.02.018; PMID 24656292)
- 42 Danish children with AS (born 1991–2009, registry-based). Mental development and autistic features in the deletion group were followed over 12 years. **Children with 15q11.2–q13 deletions were significantly less advanced than children with non-deletion AS.** — [PubMed 24656292](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24656292/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0891422214000900)

**Mertz et al. 2014b** "Eating behavior, prenatal and postnatal growth in AS" (cohort; Res Dev Disabil 35:2681–2690)
- 39 children: 12 class I deletion, 18 class II deletion, **5 paternal UPD**, 4 UBE3A mutation. **pUPD children had significantly larger birth weight and length than deletion or UBE3A children, and a significantly higher BMI than deletion children.** — [ScienceDirect S0891422214002959](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0891422214002959)
- (secondary) Children with UPD-AS show more hyperphagia than children with deletion-AS. — same source as summarised in search; see also [Genes 2021 review](https://doi.org/10.3390/genes12070987)
- Higher body mass in infancy and early toddlerhood in UPD and ICD (imprinting-centre defect) patients (cohort). — [Brennan et al. 2015, Am J Med Genet A, PMID 25402239](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25402239/). (Secondary: "almost all patients with ID or UPD become overweight before 44 months of age.") — [GeneReviews-based summaries / Genes 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/genes12070987)
- GeneReviews (2017 version): deletions have a lower BMI than UPD or ID. — [Dagli, Mueller, Williams, GeneReviews "Angelman Syndrome" (2017 PDF copy)](https://ern-ithaca.eu/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Dagli_Angelman_general_GeneRev2017.pdf); live version [NBK1144](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK1144/)

**Keute et al. 2021** (natural-history cohort; Mol Psychiatry 26:3625–3633; doi:10.1038/s41380-020-0858-6). Authors: Keute M, Miller MT, Krishnan ML, Sadhwani A, Chamberlain S, Thibert RL, Tan WH, Bird LM, Hipp JF.
- n = 250 individuals, 848 assessments (Bayley, Vineland, language and AS-specific severity scales; AS Natural History Study). **Deletion was most severe.** Deletion individuals also showed greater measurable change over time, especially in early childhood. — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41380-020-0858-6); [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/8z43c9pr)
- **"Individuals with UBE3A pathogenic variants and imprinting defects (IPD) are less affected than individuals with uniparental paternal disomy (UPD)"**. Within UBE3A variants, truncating mutations impaired more than missense. Severity order: deletion > UPD > ID ≈ UBE3A. — [Keute 2021 (summary)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41380-020-0858-6)
- Companion ASNHS epilepsy analysis (265 children): generalised motor seizures more frequent with deletion; absence seizures equally frequent in deletion and non-deletion. The probability that an ID patient has had a seizure by 60 months was only 29 % (95 % CI 6–46 %). — ["Clinical Characterization of Epilepsy in Children With AS", Pediatr Neurol 2021, PMC8500934](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8500934/)
- (secondary) UPD subtype has the lowest epilepsy frequency and the least severe epilepsy phenotype. — [Genes 2021 review](https://doi.org/10.3390/genes12070987)

**Bindels-de Heus et al. 2020** (cohort, children; Am J Med Genet A 182(1):53–63; doi:10.1002/ajmg.a.61382; ENCORE, Rotterdam)
- 100 children. **Epilepsy 82 %, significantly more in the deletion group.** Early epilepsy onset was linked to worse motor, cognitive and receptive-language outcomes. Non-convulsive status epilepticus was frequent, and crouch gait was frequent in older children. Head circumference was lower, significantly so in deletion. **Weight-for-height was raised in all subtypes and significantly higher in non-deletion.** Hyperphagia was frequent in all subtypes. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ajmg.a.61382); [ERN-ITHACA PDF](https://ern-ithaca.eu/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Bindels_Angelman_child_AJMG2020.pdf)
- Follow-up ENCORE paper, "Hyperphagia, Growth, and Puberty in Children with AS" (J Clin Med 2023;12(18):5981; cohort): parents reported **hyperphagia in about one third, in both deletion and non-deletion**. **43 % had BMI > +1 SDS, significantly more in non-deletion (p < 0.001)**. 20 % were obese (BMI > +2 SDS) with no genotype difference. Higher BMI-SDS was significantly associated with non-deletion genotype. — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-0383/12/18/5981); [PMC10532359](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10532359/)

**den Besten et al. 2021** (cross-sectional cohort, adults; Am J Med Genet A 185(1):168–181; doi:10.1002/ajmg.a.61940; PMID 33108066; ENCORE, NL)
- **Epilepsy in 57 % of adults, significantly more in the deletion group.** It harmed functioning. 45 % of those with epilepsy had been seizure-free for ≥1 year, mostly on antiseizure drugs. Medication failed to control epilepsy in 11 %. — [PubMed 33108066](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33108066/); [Wiley full text](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ajmg.a.61940)
- Behavioural data were available for 93 adults. **"Sixteen percent of the group with behavioral problems used medication, most often risperidone."** — [PubMed 33108066](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33108066/)
- **"Forty-one percent of the adults with a history of sleep problems used sleep medication, mainly melatonin with variable doses (range 0.1–5 mg)."** — [PubMed 33108066](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33108066/)
- Weight: 37 % overweight (BMI ≥25), about one third of them obese. The obesity figure is inconsistent within the paper (9 % in text vs 11.5 % in table, per search extract). Adult obesity by genotype was apparently not reported. — [Wiley full text](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ajmg.a.61940)
- The same group reports debilitating health problems in adults: constipation, reflux, visual problems, scoliosis, behavioural and sleep problems. — [Wiley full text](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ajmg.a.61940)

**Larson et al. 2015** (cohort, standardised caregiver phone interviews; Am J Med Genet A 167A(2):331–344; doi:10.1002/ajmg.a.36864; PMID 25428759; MGH Boston)
- 110 adolescents and adults, mean age 24 (range 16–50). **Active seizures 41 %, sleep dysfunction 72 %** (sleep improves with age but stays common), **constipation 85 %, overweight/obesity 32 %** (more often women), **scoliosis 50 %** (mean age at diagnosis 12; 24 % of these had surgery, more often men), **self-injury 52 %**. — [PubMed 25428759](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25428759/); [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ajmg.a.36864)
- Anxiety figures attributed to Larson 2015 **conflict between secondary sources**: "46 % (n = 48) showed some signs of anxiety" vs. "anxiety present in 57 %, rising to 71 % at ages 26–43". A UK summary gives "47 % of adolescents and 71 % of adults". Check against the full text before citing. — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/268878058_Angelman_Syndrome_in_Adulthood); [Cure Angelman UK](https://cureangelman.org.uk/behavior-and-anxiety-in-angelman-syndrome/)
- (secondary) Physical aggression/irritability increases in adulthood (Larson 2015 as cited). — [Unmet clinical needs review, Wheeler et al. 2017, Orphanet J Rare Dis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13023-017-0716-z)

**Prasad et al. 2018** (retrospective chart review; Am J Med Genet A 176(6):1327–1334; doi:10.1002/ajmg.a.38694; MGH Angelman Clinic: Prasad A, Grocott O, Parkin K, Larson A, Thibert RL)

**Further genotype data**
- Fujimoto et al. 2024 (cohort, 134 patients: 57 UBE3A, 48 UPD/ID; HGG Advances 5(4)): about one in five UPD/ID patients had no movement/balance disorder. **In UPD/ID, easily provoked laughter and hyperactivity became more pronounced with age.** Hypopigmentation and seizures differed by genotype. UBE3A/UPD/ID patients had more obesity and fewer seizures, hypopigmentation and microcephaly. — [Cell/HGG Adv](https://www.cell.com/hgg-advances/fulltext/S2666-2477(24)00082-4?rss=yes); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666247724000824)
- OMIM: UPD, ID and UBE3A patients were taller and heavier than deletion patients. — [OMIM #105830](https://omim.org/entry/105830)

**Behavioural and psychiatric profile in adolescents/adults (all genotypes)**
- **Wheeler et al. 2019** (cross-sectional survey, n = 100; J Intellect Disabil Res 63(10):1234–1247; doi:10.1111/jir.12635; PMID 31134691): anxiety concerns in **40 %**. **~70 % had a preferred caregiver and over half showed distress when separated from that caregiver.** **Deletion subtype and younger age were linked to *fewer* anxiety behaviours**, so non-deletion and older individuals had more. Sleep difficulties and aggression were consistent significant predictors of total anxiety. — [PubMed 31134691](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31134691/); [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jir.12635)
- "Parent Description of Anxiety in AS" (J Autism Dev Disord, ~2021; qualitative/cohort, n = 50): the most common anxiety trigger was **"separation from caregiver/parent."** — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-021-05238-8)
- **Global Angelman Syndrome Registry, adults** (cohort, n = 37, ages 18–46; J Autism Dev Disord 2024; PMID 38767816): **56 % showed challenging behaviour**. It was significantly associated with **sleep arousal problems** and with **lack of physical therapy**. The authors suggest sleep interventions and more physiotherapy. Small sample, preliminary. — [PubMed 38767816](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38767816/); [PMC12296864](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12296864/); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-024-06367-6)
- Aggression prevalence varies widely with method: Arron et al. 2011 (cross-syndrome questionnaire study) found **physical aggression in 73 % of AS** (vs. 70 % Cri-du-chat). Reviews cite **72 % in adults** (Challenging Behaviour Questionnaire). Other estimates over broader age ranges are 6–10 % (Adams et al.; Summers et al. 1995). Much reported aggression is low-intensity (grabbing, hair-pulling). — [Wheeler et al. 2017 review, Orphanet J Rare Dis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13023-017-0716-z); [FIND resources: Aggression in AS](https://findresources.co.uk/the-syndromes/angelman/aggression); [Tunnicliffe PhD thesis, Birmingham](https://etheses.bham.ac.uk/768/1/Tunnicliffe10PhD.pdf)
- **Aggression in AS is more likely when adult attention is low.** It is socially motivated, attention-maintained behaviour, and the hypothesis is that inappropriate social seeking drives much of the challenging behaviour in AS. (The attribution to a 2021 study comes from search extracts; probably Heald, Adams, Walls & Oliver 2021, Front Behav Neurosci 15:618271, which compared social motivation **between genetic subgroups**.) — [Heald et al. 2021, Front Behav Neurosci](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnbeh.2021.618271/full); [FIND resources](https://findresources.co.uk/the-syndromes/angelman/aggression)
- GI comorbidity as a behavioural driver: among 163 patients from two clinics, **constipation 84 %, reflux 64 %**. Self-injury and aggression correlated with GI symptoms and sleep problems (cohort). — [Glassman et al. 2017, "Prevalence of gastrointestinal symptoms in AS", PubMed 28816003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28816003/)
- Pain and medical causes (e.g., severe reflux) should be considered when a usually happy person with AS becomes persistently irritable (clinical guide). — [Univ. of Utah, AS Pediatric Primary Care Guide](https://trip.utah.edu/angelman-syndrome-pediatric-primary-care-guide/)
- Adult male, testosterone-linked aggression (case report): aggression and hypersexuality rising with age in a man with AS were controlled with **leuprolide for 9 years, followed by bilateral orchiectomy**. The authors call it the first such case and recommend it only for selected refractory cases. — [Anderson, Porwal, Sandlow 2022, Cureus 14(5):e24865, PMID 35698716](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35698716/); [PMC9184178](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9184178/)
- Sleep in AS: no aetiology-specific treatment exists. Behavioural and symptomatic drug treatment have limited effect, and melatonin is the most-used agent (review). — ["Sleep disturbance in AS patients", Orphanet J Rare Dis 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13023-024-03154-5)
- Adult parkinsonism: two young adults with AS developed intermittent severe resting tremor, cogwheel rigidity and bradykinesia in late adolescence. It was **not medication-induced** and improved dramatically with levodopa (case report). — [Harbord 2001, J Clin Neurosci, Flinders record](https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/levodopa-responsive-parkinsonism-in-adults-with-angelman-syndrome/)
- Tremor: families reported it in 66 %, but expert video review confirmed it in only 3/20 (15 %) (video cohort). — ["Myoclonic Dystonia… in AS", PMC12999045](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12999045/)

### Inferences
- For this patient, **no epilepsy, independent walking, good receptive language, BMI ~30 and strong food/sugar seeking fit the published UPD profile**: low seizure risk, better motor and communication skills, more obesity and hyperphagia. Lifetime seizure risk is lower but not zero. Absence seizures occur equally often in non-deletion, and there are reports of adult-onset seizures and NCSE.
- His **attachment to specific people (mother, grandmother, selected carers), with aggression when they withhold attention**, matches two well-documented AS features: a "preferred caregiver" with separation distress (Wheeler 2019: ~70 % / >50 %), and **attention-maintained** aggression. Both data sources suggest this is more pronounced in non-deletion and older individuals. It can be framed as part of the syndrome-specific behaviour profile, not as "bad character", and it is accessible to functional-analysis-based behavioural intervention.
- His fragmented sleep (17–21 h and 23–4/5 h) is typical of adult AS (72 % sleep dysfunction in Larson 2015). Sleep problems predict both anxiety and challenging behaviour (Wheeler 2019; registry 2024). **Treating sleep is therefore also likely to treat behaviour.**
- His hard stools matter for behaviour. Constipation (84–85 %) is linked to aggression and self-injury, so a bowel regimen belongs in the behavioural plan.
- Keute 2021 places UPD between deletion and ID/UBE3A in severity. The family should not be told that UPD is always the "mild" form.

## Key Question 2: What does the Duis et al. 2022 consensus recommend for behaviour/aggression, anxiety, ADHD-like symptoms, sleep and psychotropic drugs?

### Takeaway
The international consensus (Duis et al. 2022) is **expert consensus**, built because evidence-based literature is lacking. Working groups included psychiatry, psychology, sleep and neurology. It has a dedicated **"Management Algorithm for Behavior in AS" (Figure 4)** and states that medication management is often needed. I could not read the algorithm text in this session. Secondary summaries agree on the approach. First, look for medical and physical causes (pain, reflux, constipation, sleep, seizures) and use behavioural and communication interventions, including functional behaviour analysis. Then use medication by target symptom:
- anxiety: SSRIs or buspirone; as-needed benzodiazepines or propranolol
- hyperactivity: non-stimulant alpha-2 agonists (clonidine/guanfacine) preferred
- severe aggression or irritability: atypical antipsychotics (risperidone/aripiprazole)
- sleep: sleep hygiene and behavioural measures, then melatonin, then mirtazapine, trazodone or clonidine

- UK summary of the standards of care: sleep problems are first treated with behavioural therapy and sleep hygiene, and sleep medication can help if those fail. Contributing factors (constipation, reflux, seizures, anxiety) should be treated. Options listed: alpha-agonists (clonidine, guanfacine), antidepressants (mirtazapine, trazodone) and, in resistant cases, antipsychotics (e.g., quetiapine). — [Cure Angelman UK: Standards of Care](https://cureangelman.org.uk/our-work/standards-of-care/)
- UK expert-panel summary on behaviour and anxiety (**lay/advocacy summary of expert opinion**, not formal SoC): "Medications commonly used for anxiety, such as buspirone and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), can be helpful. The panel also discussed as-needed medication for aggression and **non-stimulant medication for hyperactivity**. Behavioural approaches, including functional behaviour analysis, are first-line. — [Cure Angelman UK: Behavior and Anxiety in AS](https://cureangelman.org.uk/behavior-and-anxiety-in-angelman-syndrome/)
- University of Utah primary care guide (**clinical guide/expert opinion**): treatment usually combines behaviour management with medication when behavioural management alone fails. "Although use is not well studied in children with AS, **risperidone and aripiprazole** are FDA-approved for irritability in children 6–17 with autism and may be helpful." SSRIs may help some individuals. Atypical antipsychotics may help self-injury, aggression or hyperactivity not controlled by stimulants. Clonidine, trazodone and mirtazapine are listed for sleep. Treating anxiety should be a priority. — [Utah AS Pediatric Primary Care Guide](https://trip.utah.edu/angelman-syndrome-pediatric-primary-care-guide/)
- Expert review (**narrative review**): communication-focused speech therapy and behavioural interventions addressing attention-seeking may reduce aggression and self-injury. "Treatments for anxiety including **buspirone, clonazepam, and propranolol** may reduce aggressive behaviors, and **atypical antipsychotics may be considered in severe cases**." Behavioural therapy can treat sleep problems driven by bedtime separation. — ["Current and emerging treatment options for AS", Expert Rev Neurother 2023;23(9), doi:10.1080/14737175.2023.2245568](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14737175.2023.2245568)
- Related ASF documents: the ASF Clinical Care Tool Kit (07/2023) and the ASF seizure treatment guidelines (Nov 2024). — [ASF Clinical Care Tool Kit PDF](https://www.angelman.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/ASF_ClinicalCareToolKit_072023.pdf); [ASF Seizure Treatment Guidelines Nov 2024 PDF](https://angelman.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/ANSF_Seizure-Treatment-Guidelines_Nov2024.pdf)

### Inferences
- Across all sources the consensus logic is the same, and it maps onto this patient as follows.
  1. **Rule out and treat medical drivers**: constipation (he has hard stools), reflux, dental pain, sleep disorder (consider polysomnography for sleep apnoea, especially with BMI 30), and unrecognised seizures/NCSE.
  2. **Function-based behaviour plan**: functional behaviour assessment; give attention on a schedule rather than in response to aggression; train an AAC/communication replacement behaviour; structure and predictability; physical activity and physiotherapy, which the registry links to less challenging behaviour.
  3. **Then targeted drugs**: anxiety/separation distress → SSRI or buspirone; hyperactivity/restlessness → alpha-2 agonist (guanfacine/clonidine) before stimulants; severe, dangerous aggression → atypical antipsychotic (risperidone/aripiprazole); sleep → melatonin first, then mirtazapine/trazodone/clonidine.
- Because he is obese and hyperphagic, the antipsychotic choice should take metabolic risk into account. This is general psychopharmacology, not AS-specific evidence: risperidone carries more weight-gain risk than aripiprazole.

### Gaps
- **The exact wording and steps of Duis 2022 Figure 4 (behaviour algorithm) and the psychiatry section** (named drugs, first- vs second-line, dose advice, any "start low, go slow" statement) could **not be verified** because the full text was not reachable. My unverified recollection: the algorithm starts with medical causes and behavioural intervention, then uses symptom-targeted drugs (SSRIs for anxiety, alpha-2 agonists/stimulants for hyperactivity, atypical antipsychotics for aggression). ## Key Question 3: Which psychotropics are used in AS cohorts, with what effects and side effects, and are there AS-specific warnings or drug sensitivities?

### Takeaway
**There are no RCTs of any psychotropic for behaviour in AS.** The only AS RCT for a symptomatic drug is melatonin (n = 8). Real-world use is documented only descriptively:
- adults (NL): 16 % of those with behavioural problems took medication, mostly risperidone; 41 % of those with sleep problems took sleep medication, mostly melatonin 0.1–5 mg
- children: daily benzodiazepines 47.5 % and other psychotropics 75 % in one sleep cohort

AS-specific cautions:
- sodium-channel antiseizure drugs (**carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine**), **vigabatrin** and **tiagabine** can worsen AS seizures
- low melatonin doses are advised because many patients are poor CYP1A2 metabolisers
- atypical benzodiazepine responses and high vagal tone (bradycardia) under anaesthesia
- sedating drugs and antiseizure drugs suppress respiratory rate in sleep
- levodopa-responsive parkinsonism can appear in young adults, which matters when dopamine-blocking drugs are used

### Cited Findings

**Real-world use (cohorts)**
- Adults, ENCORE NL (cohort): **16 % of those with behavioural problems used medication, most often risperidone. 41 % of those with sleep problems used sleep medication, mainly melatonin (0.1–5 mg).** — [den Besten et al. 2021, PMID 33108066](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33108066/)
- Children (sleep/respiration cohort): **daily benzodiazepine use 47.5 %, daily use of other psychotropic drugs 75 %**. Medication use, particularly antiseizure drugs, was associated with **suppressed respiratory rate during sleep**. — ["Lower respiratory rate during sleep in children with AS compared to age-matched controls", PMC11980168](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11980168)
- Epilepsy questionnaire study (Thibert et al. 2009, Epilepsia; cohort/survey): **valproic acid and clonazepam were the most commonly prescribed**; lamotrigine and levetiracetam showed similar efficacy and tolerability. — [Thibert et al. 2009, doi:10.1111/j.1528-1167.2009.02108.x](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1528-1167.2009.02108.x)

**Sleep drugs**
- **Melatonin, Braam et al. 2008 (RCT, n = 8 children with chronic insomnia; 2.5 or 5 mg by age vs placebo):** sleep onset advanced 28 min, sleep latency −32 min, **total sleep +56 min**, nights with waking down from 3.1 to 1.6/week. **The authors advise low doses because many children with AS were poor CYP1A2 metabolisers.** — [Braam et al. 2008, J Intellect Disabil Res, doi:10.1111/j.1365-2788.2008.01119_11.x](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1365-2788.2008.01119_11.x); [ResearchGate RCT](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5317470_Melatonin_for_Chronic_Insomnia_in_Angelman_Syndrome_A_Randomized_Placebo-Controlled_Trial). Limitation: subjective outcome measures. — [Sleep disturbance in AS review 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13023-024-03154-5)
- **Mirtazapine** (retrospective chart review, 8 paediatric cases): improved sleep onset and night waking in 7/8. — [J Clin Sleep Med 2020, doi:10.5664/jcsm.8284](https://jcsm.aasm.org/doi/10.5664/jcsm.8284)
- Antihistamines (diphenhydramine, niaprazine) are used, with limited evidence (review). — [Sleep disturbance in AS patients, OJRD 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13023-024-03154-5)

**Behaviour drugs (no AS-specific trials)**
- Risperidone and aripiprazole: "not well studied" in AS; support is extrapolated from autism irritability approvals (clinical guide). — [Utah guide](https://trip.utah.edu/angelman-syndrome-pediatric-primary-care-guide/)
- Older review: "in the authors' experience, neuroleptics have not been useful in controlling behavioral problems or in increasing attention" in AS (**expert opinion**). — [Pelc, Cheron, Dan 2008, "Behavior and neuropsychiatric manifestations in AS", Neuropsychiatr Dis Treat](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.2147/ndt.s2749)
- SSRIs, buspirone, as-needed benzodiazepines or propranolol for anxiety; non-stimulants for hyperactivity (expert panel). — [Cure Angelman UK](https://cureangelman.org.uk/behavior-and-anxiety-in-angelman-syndrome/)
- Buspirone, clonazepam and propranolol may reduce aggression; atypical antipsychotics in severe cases (review). — [Expert Rev Neurother 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14737175.2023.2245568)
- Preclinical only: fluoxetine partly restored glucocorticoid signalling and rescued anxiety-like behaviour in an AS mouse model. — [Cure Angelman UK summary](https://cureangelman.org.uk/behavior-and-anxiety-in-angelman-syndrome/)
- Hormonal (case report): leuprolide (GnRH agonist) controlled age-related, testosterone-linked aggression and hypersexuality in an adult male with AS for 9 years. — [Cureus 2022, PMC9184178](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9184178/)
- Non-AS context (autism/ID): aripiprazole causes significant weight gain in under-18s, though less than risperidone or olanzapine. Metformin reduced antipsychotic weight gain versus placebo in autistic youth on risperidone or aripiprazole. *Indirect evidence, not AS.* — [BJPsych Advances: aripiprazole in ASD](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjpsych-advances/article/aripiprazole-in-autism-spectrum-disorder-current-evidence-for-use/8125E350F3F5CEED0B6F43AA4E660AA3); [Psychopharmacology Institute: risperidone vs aripiprazole](https://psychopharmacologyinstitute.com/section/treatment-of-irritability-in-asd-risperidone-and-aripiprazole-2695-5381/)

**AS-specific warnings and drug sensitivities**
- **Antiseizure drugs that can worsen AS seizures:** carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine (sodium-channel blockers) can aggravate absence and myoclonic seizures, and vigabatrin can paradoxically increase seizure frequency. — [StatPearls: Angelman Syndrome](https://www.statpearls.com/point-of-care/17553); [Angelman Syndrome News: Controlling Seizures in AS](https://angelmansyndromenews.com/health-insights/controlling-seizures-in-angelman-syndrome/). The Utah guide lists **vigabatrin, carbamazepine and tiagabine** as possibly worsening certain seizure types. — [Utah guide](https://trip.utah.edu/angelman-syndrome-pediatric-primary-care-guide/). Sources differ slightly in which drugs they list. Thibert 2009 is the usual primary citation, but this could not be verified from its text here.
- **Relevance to psychiatry:** carbamazepine and oxcarbazepine are sometimes used as "mood stabilisers" for aggression in ID. In AS they carry this seizure-aggravation warning (inference from the above).
- **Anaesthesia/sedation** (case series and review): reports of **malignant bradydysrhythmias** under anaesthesia and generally **high vagal tone** in AS. **Atypical responses to benzodiazepines** occur (a 16-year-old needed flumazenil), consistent with reduced benzodiazepine binding in mouse models. Craniofacial features can complicate airway management. In the series, 6 patients had 18 procedures, mostly uncomplicated. — [Anesth Pain Med 2018 case series, PMID 29696118](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29696118); [Brieflands full text](https://brieflands.com/journals/aapm/articles/57826); ["AS and anaesthetic considerations", PMC5296816](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5296816/); ASF says anaesthesia risk is generally no different from other patients — [ASF Clinical Care Tool Kit](https://www.angelman.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/ASF_ClinicalCareToolKit_072023.pdf)
- **Respiratory depression:** antiseizure and other sedating drugs were associated with lower respiratory rates during sleep in children with AS. — [PMC11980168](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11980168)
- **Movement disorders in adults:** levodopa-responsive parkinsonism (not medication-induced) in young adults with AS (case report). — [Harbord 2001](https://researchnow.flinders.edu.au/en/publications/levodopa-responsive-parkinsonism-in-adults-with-angelman-syndrome/)

### Inferences
- The evidence for psychotropics in AS behaviour is at best **expert opinion and descriptive cohorts**. The final document should say so plainly. Any trial should be an individual, monitored n-of-1 trial with defined targets: aggression frequency, sleep log, weight/BMI, waist, lipids, glucose and prolactin with antipsychotics, and ECG where relevant.
- For **this patient** (obese, hyperphagic, sugar-seeking): risperidone (the most-used drug in the Dutch adult cohort) carries a notable risk of weight gain and more appetite. Aripiprazole, or low-dose risperidone with metabolic monitoring and possibly metformin, would be the usual general-psychiatry considerations. This is extrapolated, not AS-specific.
- Benzodiazepine sensitivity is mechanistically tied to **GABRB3 loss in deletion cases**. In paternal UPD, GABRB3 is present on both (paternal) chromosomes, so this mechanism may matter less. However, the clinical reports did not stratify by genotype, so caution still applies (mechanistic inference; not verified in UPD).
- Because adult AS can develop parkinsonism, dopamine-blocking antipsychotics need monitoring for extrapyramidal signs (rigidity, tremor, bradykinesia). This is a clinical inference.
- Melatonin should start low (e.g., 0.5–2 mg; Braam's CYP1A2 finding; the Dutch adult range was 0.1–5 mg). Prolonged-release melatonin may suit his early-morning waking better than immediate release (inference).

### Gaps
- **No AS-specific data were found** on SSRIs (efficacy or activation side effects), guanfacine/clonidine, stimulants (methylphenidate) or mood stabilisers (valproate, lithium, topiramate) for behaviour. Targeted searches for AS + ADHD medication returned only general ADHD and autism literature.
- No AS-specific case reports of risperidone or aripiprazole effects or side effects were found.
- No source found on **valproate-specific adverse effects in AS** (tremor, encephalopathy, weight gain). From memory, valproate tremor and encephalopathy reports exist, but this is unverified.
- Not verified: the full content of Thibert 2009 on which drugs worsened seizures, and of the ASF seizure guidelines (Nov 2024).

## Key Question 4: What is the status of the disease-modifying pipeline (ASOs, gaboxadol, gene therapy) in Nov 2026, who is eligible (age, genotype), and what is relevant for a 26-year-old with UPD and for Germany/Europe?

### Takeaway
As of November 2026 there is **no approved disease-modifying treatment**.
- **Ultragenyx's apazunersen (GTX-102) failed Phase 3 Aspire** (topline 2 Sep 2026: Bayley-4 cognition primary endpoint and MDRI key secondary both missed). The programme's future is under review, and its Aurora basket study (which included adult UPD) is listed as active/not recruiting.
- **Ionis' obudanersen (ION582)** has FDA Breakthrough designation. Phase 3 **REVEAL** (deletion/mutation only) has completed paediatric pivotal enrolment and has EU sites, including **LMU Munich**.
- **Ionis' CHAMPION (NCT07782827)** is a new open-label Phase 3 study **specifically for paternal UPD and imprinting defects, ages 2–50**, planned to start by end of 2026; sites are not yet confirmed. **This is the single most relevant trial for a 26-year-old with UPD.**
- **Rugonersen** (Roche → Oak Hill Bio) Phase 3 **BEACON** started July 2026 (ages 1–50; deletion/mutation per one listing; topline early 2029).
- **Gaboxadol** failed Phase 3 NEPTUNE in 2020 and was dropped.
- The first AAV gene therapy (**MavriX MVX-220, ASCEND-AS**) has dosed its first patient.

### Inferences
- **Mechanistic relevance for UPD:** all three ASOs (apazunersen, obudanersen, rugonersen) reduce the paternal UBE3A antisense transcript (UBE3A-ATS) to "unsilence" the intact paternal UBE3A copy. In paternal UPD both chromosome-15 copies are paternal and intact, so the mechanism applies in principle. This is presumably why Ionis built a dedicated UPD/ID study (CHAMPION). There are **no published efficacy data in UPD yet.**
- **For this patient (26 y, UPD):** within the age window (2–50) and genotype criteria of **CHAMPION**, the most realistic trial option. Sites are unknown; it may or may not include a German or EU site. **Not eligible for REVEAL** (deletion/mutation only), and probably not for BEACON (deletion/mutation per one listing; unconfirmed). Aurora would have fitted (adults 18–64 with UPD) but is listed as not recruiting, and the Ultragenyx programme is under review after Aspire. The family could register with the Global AS Registry and FAST/ASF trial alerts, and contact the LMU/Aachen centres about CHAMPION site plans.
- Realistic expectations: even if positive, Phase 3 readouts are 2027 (REVEAL; timing unverified) to 2029 (BEACON). The Aspire failure shows that cognitive endpoints may not move, especially in older patients. Expected effects in adults are unknown. Intrathecal dosing every 12 weeks needs lumbar punctures, possibly under sedation (see the anaesthesia cautions in Q3), which is a practical burden for a strong, behaviourally challenging adult.

## Key Question 5: Which German resources are relevant for adult AS care (Angelman e.V., specialised centres, MZEB)?

### Takeaway
Germany has a dedicated **adult** AS centre: the **Angelman Zentrum Aachen (AZA)**, part of the MZEB (Medizinisches Zentrum für Erwachsene mit Behinderung) at Uniklinik RWTH Aachen. It was founded in 2021 together with **Angelman e.V.**, is listed by the ASF as an ASF clinic, and works closely with the paediatric **Angelman Zentrum München (LMU Klinikum, iSPZ Hauner)**. LMU Munich is also the first EU site of Ionis' REVEAL trial.

### Cited Findings
- **Angelman Zentrum Aachen (AZA)** at the **MZEB, Uniklinik RWTH Aachen**: founded 2021 with Angelman e.V. The MZEB has treated adults with disabilities from across Germany since Nov 2018. Outpatient care for **adults ≥18**; appointments on consecutive days possible; video consultations for known patients. Contact: **Tel. 0241 80 89 606, mzeb@ukaachen.de**. Referral via SPZ doctor, another doctor, or self-referral (the 2021 flyer mentioned a GdB-70 criterion; this may be outdated, so check by phone). — [UK Aachen: Angelman Zentrum Aachen](https://www.ukaachen.de/kliniken-institute/mzeb/fuer-patienten-und-zuweiser/angelman-zentrum-aachen/); [AZA flyer 2021 (UK Aachen)](https://www.ukaachen.de/fileadmin/files/zentren/mzeb/20211026_CG_FLY_MZEB_Angelman_Zentrum_Aachen_WEB.pdf); [AZA flyer (angelman.de)](https://angelman.de/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/AZA_WEB.pdf); [MZEB Aachen](https://www.ukaachen.de/kliniken-institute/mzeb/)
- ASF lists the "Angelman Center Aachen" as an ASF clinic that "cares for all adult patients with AS" and serves as a point of contact for diagnostics. — [ASF: Angelman Center Aachen](https://angelman.org/asf-clinics/angelman-center-aachen/)
- **Angelman Zentrum München** (LMU Klinikum, iSPZ, child and developmental neurology): children and adolescents up to 18; cooperates with Aachen. — [LMU Klinikum: Angelman Zentrum München](https://www.lmu-klinikum.de/ispz/schwerpunkte/kinderneurologie-und-entwicklungsneurologie/angelman-zentrum-munchen/92177eec075dcc14)
- **LMU Klinikum München is the first EU enrolment site for REVEAL** (April 2026). — [Ionis REVEAL EU letter](https://angelman.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Ionis-REVEAL-sites-EU_community-letter.pdf)
- **Angelman e.V.**: German parent self-help association; contact as-info(at)angelman.de; maintains a page on Angelman centres. — [Angelman e.V.: Angelman-Zentren](https://angelman.de/hilfe-fuer-eltern/angelman-zentren/); [Epikurier 3/2022: "Angelman – ein Syndrom und sein Verein"](https://www.epikurier.de/archiv/ausgabe-32022/angelman-ein-syndrom-und-sein-verein/)

- **Global Angelman Syndrome Registry** (web-based, caregiver-reported; NCT05293184) is open internationally and feeds adult studies. — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05293184](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05293184)

### Inferences
- For this patient, the **AZA/MZEB Aachen** is the natural place for a syndrome-specific adult work-up: behaviour and medication review, sleep, metabolic and obesity care, constipation, bone health. It can also help with the group-home transition and advise on trial participation (CHAMPION). MZEBs exist in other German regions too and provide multidisciplinary adult disability care under §119c SGB V, but only Aachen is Angelman-specific (inference; general MZEB legal basis not verified here).
- Angelman e.V. is the obvious channel for German-language family support and for news on CHAMPION sites in Germany.

### Gaps
- No German-language AS-specific medication guidance (e.g., from Angelman e.V. or AZA) was found or retrieved.
- Current AZA referral criteria and waiting times are unverified; the 2021 flyer may be outdated.
- I did not check whether other German MZEBs or university centres (e.g., ZSE Essen, given the Horsthemke/Gillessen-Kaesbach imprinting tradition) run AS-specific adult clinics.
