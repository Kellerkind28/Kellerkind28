## 1. What is the nature of sleep problems in AS, and what do systematic reviews and cohorts report, especially about adults and the course with age?

### Takeaway
Sleep problems affect most people with AS (about 70–85% in childhood cohorts). Typical features are short total sleep, low sleep efficiency, night and early-morning waking, arousal disorders, and heavy use of sleep aids. Underlying mechanisms include an abnormal circadian/melatonin profile and abnormal sleep EEG. Problems often improve with age, but more than half of adults still have them. In adults, sleep arousal is linked to challenging behaviour, which supports the family's goal that better night sleep should reduce irritability and aggression.

### Inferences
- The patient's pattern (sleeps 17:00–21:00, then 23:00–04:00/05:00, awake afterwards, ~7 h in total) fits the AS profile of reduced total sleep and early waking. The long late-afternoon/early-evening sleep probably uses up homeostatic sleep pressure and may also delay or fragment the main sleep period. This is reasoning from general sleep physiology, not from AS-specific data.
- Adult data (den Besten 2021, Larson 2015, the registry study) suggest that persistent sleep problems at 26 are common, not unusual. The registry link between sleep arousal and challenging behaviour supports the family's goal. It is cross-sectional and does not prove causality.
- Most data are caregiver questionnaires in children. Objective adult data (actigraphy or PSG) are almost absent.

## 2. Melatonin: RCT evidence and effect sizes in AS and ID, dosing (low vs standard dose, IR vs PR, timing), adult dose, German availability and off-label status

### Takeaway
Melatonin is the best-studied and most-used sleep agent in AS. The randomised evidence is one tiny RCT in 8 children (Braam 2008), and the broader ID meta-analysis shows about −34 min sleep latency and +50 min total sleep time. Loss of effect over weeks is common and is attributed to slow melatonin clearance (CYP1A2). This argues for **low doses given early in the evening**, and for cutting the dose, not raising it, when the effect fades. In Germany, every medicinal melatonin product is prescription-only, and their labelled uses (Circadin ≥55 y; Slenyto 2–18 y with ASD/SMS) do not cover a 26-year-old, so use would be **off-label**. Food supplements are limited to about 1 mg per portion for the EU health claim, and the BfR warns against long-term use without medical advice.

### Cited Findings
**AS-specific trials**
- Braam W, Didden R, Smits MG, Curfs LM. "Melatonin for chronic insomnia in Angelman syndrome: a randomized placebo-controlled trial." *J Child Neurol* 2008 (PubMed 18539989). **[RCT, children, n = 8]** Melatonin 2.5 mg or 5 mg depending on age, after a 1-week baseline. Reported versus placebo: sleep onset advanced by **28 min**, sleep latency reduced by **32 min**, total sleep time increased by **56 min**, and nights with wakes per week fell from 3.1 to 1.6. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5317470_Melatonin_for_Chronic_Insomnia_in_Angelman_Syndrome_A_Randomized_Placebo-Controlled_Trial); [Unbound Medicine/PRIME PubMed 18539989](https://wwww.unboundmedicine.com/medline/citation/18539989/Melatonin_for_chronic_insomnia_in_Angelman_syndrome:_a_randomized_placebo_controlled_trial_)
- Braam W, et al. "Melatonin is effective in treating sleep problems in Angelman syndrome but problems in metabolising melatonin may be part of the Angelman phenotype." *J Intellect Disabil Res* 2008 (conference abstract). After several weeks, melatonin **lost its beneficial effect in most AS patients**. The authors hypothesise that disturbed melatonin metabolism is part of the AS phenotype. — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1365-2788.2008.01119_11.x); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/23481376_Melatonin_is_effective_in_treating_sleep_problems_in_Angelman_syndrome_but_problems_in_metabolising_melatonin_may_be_part_of_the_Angelman_phenotype)
- Zhdanova IV, Wurtman RJ, Wagstaff J. "Effects of a low dose of melatonin on sleep in children with Angelman syndrome." *J Pediatr Endocrinol Metab* 1999 (PubMed 10392349). **[open trial, children 2–10 y, n = 13]** Home actigraphy. A moderate rise in circulating melatonin significantly reduced motor activity during sleep and promoted sleep. There was no placebo control. — [PubMed 10392349](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10392349/?dopt=Abstract); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Effects-of-a-Low-Dose-of-Melatonin-on-Sleep-in-with-Zhdanova-Wurtman/57868a006662bd3e20d2f39745961d84c47792bb)

### Inferences
- For a 26-year-old man with AS, both medicinal products (Circadin, Slenyto) would be **off-label**: wrong age for Circadin, and wrong age and indication for Slenyto. The S3 guideline's 3-month limit and its advice against long-term use apply to primary insomnia in the general population. They do not directly address circadian/neurodevelopmental sleep disorders in ID, where long-term use is common practice (den Besten 2021: melatonin is the main sleep drug in Dutch adults with AS). The final document should say this explicitly.
- Two features of the AS evidence point to a **low dose** (in the 0.5–1 mg range, roughly what supplements contain) given **early in the evening**, with dose reduction rather than escalation if the effect fades: the frequent loss of effect at 2.5–5 mg (Braam 2008, Braam 2010), and the dim-light-melatonin-onset-oriented timing used in circadian disorders. The specific dose range is an inference from the sources above and the Spruyt "doses should be low" remark. **No AS adult dose-finding study exists.**
- For this patient, giving melatonin in the evening while the 17:00–21:00 sleep persists may simply shift or deepen the nap. Melatonin is more likely to help if paired with removing the early-evening sleep period (see §5).
- PR melatonin (Circadin, Slenyto) targets sleep maintenance and early waking, which suits a pattern of waking at 04:00/05:00. Immediate-release (IR) melatonin acts mainly on sleep onset and phase. There is no AS-specific head-to-head comparison.

## 4. Other sleep agents in AS and ID: clonidine, trazodone, mirtazapine, gabapentin, pipamperone/melperone, quetiapine, antihistamines, benzodiazepines/Z-drugs, iron, orexin antagonists (daridorexant)

### Takeaway
Apart from melatonin, there is **no controlled trial of any sleep drug in AS**. Mirtazapine has one 8-patient pediatric chart review (7/8 improved), and weight gain and hyperphagia were the main side effects, which is a problem for this obese, sugar-craving patient. Clonidine, trazodone and gabapentin are widely used in AS (clonidine in ~15% of the US Natural History cohort), with only anecdotal or case-series support. ## 5. Behavioural and non-pharmacological interventions in AS (parent training, routines, chronotherapy, light therapy, nap removal, daytime activity), and practical relevance for a biphasic 17:00–21:00 sleep pattern

### Takeaway
Behavioural interventions are recommended as the core of AS sleep management, but the evidence is weak: one 5-child multiple-baseline study (Allen 2013) and two systematic reviews calling it "provisional but weak". A 2026 scoping review found only three eligible studies. Their common elements were consistent bedtime routines, gradually adjusted sleep schedules and parent coaching. Bright light therapy is feasible in adults with severe ID, although the only study measured mood, not sleep. No study addresses an AS adult with an early-evening sleep period like this patient's.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences (practical, not directly evidence-based for AS adults)
- For the patient's biphasic pattern, standard sleep physiology and the general behavioural principles in the reviews above suggest:
  - prevent or gradually shorten the 17:00–21:00 sleep by planning an engaging, physically active, brightly lit activity in that window;
  - if prevention is not feasible, cap the nap and move it earlier (e.g. a short early-afternoon rest);
  - hold a fixed wake time, including on weekends, with bright light or outdoor time in the morning;
  - aim for a later, consolidated bedtime (about 21:30–22:00), with a calm, dark, cool room and a fixed routine;
  - treat 04:00/05:00 waking with low-stimulation responses (dim light, no food, sweets or screens as reinforcement), especially given the attention-seeking aggression and sugar craving.

  These are reasoned recommendations, not tested in AS adults, and should be framed as such.
- Light timing matters. Morning bright light advances the clock, which risks even earlier waking if the underlying problem is an advanced phase. Bright light in the late afternoon/early evening (around 16:00–19:00) helps keep him awake through the nap window and may delay the clock somewhat, which suits an early-waking pattern. This is general chronobiology, not sourced in AS, so the writer should caveat it.
- A sleep/activity diary for 2–4 weeks, ideally with actigraphy, before and after changes is the minimum way to judge any intervention.

## 6. Medical causes of poor sleep to rule out: sleep apnoea (obesity), reflux, constipation, pain, nocturnal seizures/EEG abnormalities

### Takeaway
Constipation (85% in adolescents and adults) and overweight/obesity (32%) are very common in adult AS, and reflux is among the most common GI complaints. Obstructive sleep apnoea is rarely reported as a group prevalence (about 2% sleep-related breathing problems in the Agar meta-analysis, second-hand). However, an adult case report showed severe OSA, and obesity is a general OSA risk factor, so a sleep study is reasonable at BMI ~30.5. Sleep EEG is abnormal in AS, but in adults sleep problems were not related to epilepsy severity.

### Cited Findings
- Constipation was significant in **85%**, overweight or obesity was present in **32%** (disproportionately women), and sleep dysfunction in 72% of 110 adolescents and adults with AS. **[cohort]** — [Larson et al., AJMG A 2015 (Ovid abstract)](https://www.ovid.com/journals/ajmga/abstract/10.1002/ajmg.a.36864~angelman-syndrome-in-adulthood?redirectionsource=fulltextview)

### Inferences
- A reasonable work-up for this patient, drawn from the sources above:
  - screen for OSA (snoring or apnoea observations, then home polygraphy or PSG), given BMI ~30.5 and the adult AS case report;
  - check ferritin, CRP and blood count;
  - assess and treat constipation actively, since abdominal discomfort can cause night waking and irritability;
  - look for reflux signs (night coughing, pain behaviour after lying down, dental erosion);
  - look for pain sources (teeth, musculoskeletal);

- OSA matters for drug choice. Sedatives that depress respiration (benzodiazepines, Z-drugs, high-dose sedating antipsychotics) could worsen undiagnosed OSA. This is general pharmacology, not sourced in this session.

## 7. Which sleep medication choices fit an obese, constipated patient who may also be started on an antipsychotic?

### Takeaway
The retrieved evidence favours a hierarchy: low-dose melatonin first (weight-neutral, no constipation signal, AS and ID data, but off-label in Germany at 26). Then consider daridorexant (on-label for adult insomnia, minimal ID data) or clonidine (no weight signal, but blood-pressure effects). Avoid or deprioritise mirtazapine (hyperphagia and weight gain shown in AS), sedating antihistamines and pipamperone/melperone (S3 advises against), and benzodiazepines/Z-drugs. Most of this hierarchy is inference from the findings in §§2–4, not a published AS algorithm.

### Cited Findings
- Mirtazapine: hyperphagia and weight gain were the most common side effects in AS children. — [JCSM 2020](https://jcsm.aasm.org/doi/10.5664/jcsm.8284)
- Clonidine: hypotension, dizziness and rebound hypertension. — [Frontiers in Sleep 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sleep/articles/10.3389/frsle.2024.1389052/full)
- Daridorexant in adults with ID: no side effects, and sustained effect over 6–14 months in 3 of 5 patients. — [Drugs in Context 2026](https://www.drugsincontext.com/real-world-experience-with-daridorexant-for-insomnia-in-adults-with-intellectual-disabilities-a-retrospective-single-centre-case-series/)
- S3 2025: sedating antihistamines (grade A against), melperone, pipamperone and antipsychotics are not recommended for insomnia. — [PTAheute 2025](https://www.ptaheute.de/aktuelles/2025/05/07/schlafstoerungen-was-empfiehlt-die-neue-leitlinie)
- Melatonin is metabolised by CYP1A2, and slow metabolisers lose response at standard doses. — [Braam et al., JIDR 2010](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1365-2788.2010.01283.x)
- Obesity (32%) and constipation (85%) are common in adult AS. — [Larson et al., AJMG A 2015](https://www.ovid.com/journals/ajmga/abstract/10.1002/ajmg.a.36864~angelman-syndrome-in-adulthood?redirectionsource=fulltextview)

### Inferences
- If an antipsychotic is started for aggression, prefer an evening dose of the antipsychotic and avoid adding a second sedating antipsychotic or quetiapine for sleep. The rationales are additive metabolic risk, additive anticholinergic constipation for agents with anticholinergic activity, and the S3 position.
